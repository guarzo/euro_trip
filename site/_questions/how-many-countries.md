## Why it matters

Fourteen days sounds like a lot until you subtract the travel. Two of those days are transatlantic flights, and each intercity move costs the better part of a day once checkout, transit, and check-in are counted. What remains is roughly eleven usable days.

Three countries over eleven usable days means about three and a half days each, with two of them lost to moving. Two countries means five or six days each, with one move.

**One caveat before the options: the number of countries matters less than the number of bases.** Rome, Florence, and Naples are three cities in one country and still three unpacking cycles. Barcelona and Madrid are two bases inside Spain. Counting countries flatters an itinerary that is actually quite demanding — see [Pace]({{ '/questions/pace/' | relative_url }}) for the version of this question that actually governs the days.

## The options

### 1. Three countries

Breadth. On a first trip to Europe there is a real argument for it: you don't know yet which of these places the family will love, and sampling widely is how you find out. It also produces a better story — three countries sounds like an adventure in a way two does not.

The cost is that you are always slightly behind. Three and a half days in a city is enough to see the headline sights and nothing else. You never have the day where you wander with no plan, which is the day people actually remember.

### 2. Two countries

Depth. Five or six days per country supports a base plus a day trip, or two bases at a relaxed pace. There is room for a repeat visit to something everyone liked, and room for a day where nothing is scheduled.

The cost is obvious: you don't see the third country. On a trip this far from home, that can feel like leaving something on the table.

## My recommendation

**Two countries, three or four bases.** The trip is fourteen days, not twenty-one, and the difference between "we saw Italy and Spain properly" and "we passed through Greece, Italy, and Spain" is mostly in favor of the first.

The exception is the **northern arc**, where this calculus breaks down: London, Paris, and Amsterdam are three countries but the moves between them are 2–3 hour trains, city center to city center, with no airport overhead at all. Three countries costs about half a day of travel total. That is the one configuration where three countries is as relaxed as two would be elsewhere — which is a significant point in the northern arc's favor in [Which arc?]({{ '/questions/which-arc/' | relative_url }}).

## What would change my mind

- **If the northern arc wins**, this question mostly dissolves — three countries there is cheap.
- **If the family's instinct is "we may never come back"** — that is a legitimate reason to prioritize breadth, and it is a values call, not a logistics one.
- **If anyone strongly wants a rest day**, two countries is the only version that reliably has one.
