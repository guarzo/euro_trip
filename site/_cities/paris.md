## In winter

January highs are around 7&nbsp;°C (45&nbsp;°F), and **the sun sets at about 4:55&nbsp;PM in late December.** Grey, frequently wet, and dark by the time most people would think about an afternoon activity.

Say that plainly, because it changes how the days work: in Paris in winter you get roughly one outdoor block and one indoor block per day, not three. The city is well set up for it — the Louvre, the Orsay, and the Orangerie are all winter-proof and all enormous — but a plan built around walking the city will not survive contact with a wet January afternoon.

The compensations are real. Christmas markets run at the Tuileries and elsewhere through early January. And winter is genuinely low season for the two worst queues in the city: the Eiffel Tower and the Louvre are both approachable in a way they are not in July.

## Why go

The Louvre is the largest art museum in the world and the Musée d'Orsay, in a converted railway station, holds the best Impressionist collection anywhere. Sainte-Chapelle's upper chapel is fifteen windows of thirteenth-century glass and takes about twenty minutes to see, and it is one of the most startling interiors in Europe.

Paris also anchors the northern arc logistically. From here London is 2h20 by Eurostar and Amsterdam about 3h20 — both city center to city center, no airport, no security theater. If the trip goes north, Paris is the hinge it turns on.

The honest counterpoint: beyond the darkness, Paris in January is the least atmospheric version of itself. The café terraces that define the city are heated and plastic-wrapped or empty, and the parks are bare. It is a museum trip in winter, and that is a different holiday from the one people picture.

## Where to stay

**Saint-Germain-des-Prés**, in the 6th, is consistently the calmest, most walkable family base — close to the Louvre-Orsay-Latin Quarter loop and the Luxembourg Gardens, at a price that reflects it.

**Le Marais**, in the 3rd and 4th, is this page's own Day 3 neighborhood, and staying there keeps that walk on foot rather than by Métro. Buildings here are old, though, and narrow stairs with no elevator are common — worth asking about before booking with heavy winter luggage.

## Eat

- **Croissants and pain au chocolat** — from a bakery with a queue of locals, eaten immediately, not carried back to the room.
- **Steak frites** — the default bistro order, and the thing bistros do best.
- **Crêpes** — street crêpes for sugar and lemon, a proper crêperie for savory galettes.
- **Falafel in the Marais** — the famous version on Rue des Rosiers, eaten standing on the pavement.
- **Hot chocolate at a salon de thé** — thick, dark, served in a jug. The correct response to a dark wet afternoon.

## Getting here / onward

Charles de Gaulle (CDG) has extensive direct US service year-round; Orly handles more European and domestic traffic. The RER B from CDG reaches the center in about 35 minutes.

Onward by rail: London 2h20 and Amsterdam about 3h20, both by Eurostar, both city center to city center. Barcelona is about 6h45 by direct train — long, but competitive with flying once airport time is counted. By air: Rome about 2 hours, Athens about 3h30.
