## In winter

January highs are around 10&nbsp;°C (50&nbsp;°F) — the coldest in Italy on this list — and it is damp and grey more often than not. Florence sits in a river basin ringed by hills, which traps the chill and the fog.

The compensation is specific and large: the Uffizi and the Accademia in January are a different experience from the same rooms in July. In summer you shuffle past the Botticellis in a press of people. In winter you can stand in front of the *Birth of Venus* for as long as you want. If the art is why you would go to Florence, winter is unambiguously the better time.

Everything else about the city is unaffected by season. It is small — you can cross the historic center on foot in half an hour — so bad weather costs you less here than in a sprawling city.

## Why go

Renaissance art at a density that exists nowhere else, in a city compact enough to see properly in two days. The Duomo's dome is still an engineering marvel six hundred years on, and climbing it is the rare tourist obligation that earns itself.

**The honest part: two days is enough, and Florence is the most skippable stop on this list.** If the trip is running tight, this is the first city to cut. It is spectacular, but it is also the one place where you can see the essential things in 48 hours and leave without regret — which is not true of Rome, Barcelona, or London. It also works as a day trip from Rome by high-speed rail, which is the compromise if nobody wants to give it up entirely.

## Eat

- **Bistecca alla fiorentina** — a thick T-bone, grilled rare, sold by weight and meant to be shared. The city's signature dish.
- **Lampredotto** — tripe sandwich from a street cart. A genuine Florentine thing, and better than it sounds.
- **Schiacciata** — flat salted bread, split and stuffed, the standard fast lunch.
- **Ribollita** — bread and bean soup, thick enough to stand a spoon in. A winter dish that is actually in season.

## Getting here / onward

Florence's own airport is small; most arrivals come by train. Santa Maria Novella station sits at the edge of the historic center, and everything is walkable from it.

Onward by rail: Rome about 1.5 hours, Venice about 2 hours, Milan about 2 hours — all high-speed. That rail position is what makes Florence viable as a day trip rather than an overnight stop, if the schedule tightens.
