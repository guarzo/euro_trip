## In winter

January highs are around 7&nbsp;°C (45&nbsp;°F) — the coldest city in Italy on this list by a wide margin — and sunset is around 4:40&nbsp;PM. It is damp, foggy, and often raw in a way that the drier Mediterranean cities are not.

**This is the one Italian city here rated mixed rather than good, and the honest reason is flooding.** Winter is peak *acqua alta* season, the periodic high tide that pushes water into St. Mark's Square and the lowest-lying streets. The city's MOSE flood barriers, fully operational since 2020, now close the lagoon inlets ahead of the highest tides and have meaningfully cut how often the square actually floods — but they are not a guarantee, and a bad week can still put raised walkways across the piazza. Pack boots you don't mind getting wet.

The counterweight is real: winter fog rolling off the canals at dawn is one of the more atmospheric things on this entire list, and the crowds and the cruise-ship traffic that overwhelm Venice in summer are simply gone. You can stand in an empty campo in January that would be shoulder to shoulder in July.

## Why go

No other city on this list is built the way Venice is built. There are no cars, the streets are water, and the whole place still runs on foot and boat exactly as it has for centuries. St. Mark's Basilica and the Doge's Palace sit on the single most theatrical square in Europe, and the Grand Canal seen from a vaporetto at dusk needs no further argument made for it.

**The honest part: this is not being pushed as a must-do.** It earns a place on the list rather than a place on the itinerary. If the arc already runs through northern Italy and someone genuinely wants it, two nights is enough to see the essentials without betting the trip's weather luck on it.

## Eat

- **Cicchetti** — Venice's version of tapas: small plates at a standing bar, usually with a glass of wine, in the bars around Rialto and Cannaregio.
- **Sarde in saor** — sardines marinated with onions, pine nuts, and raisins, a Venetian original that tastes better cold and rested a day.
- **Risotto al nero di seppia** — squid-ink risotto, black and briny, a lagoon dish that doesn't travel well outside it.
- **Bacaro crawl** — an evening of moving between small wine bars rather than sitting down to one long dinner, which is how Venetians actually eat out.

## Getting here / onward

Marco Polo Airport has direct US service in season; there is no train station at the airport itself, so the connection into the city is a bus (about 20 minutes to Piazzale Roma) or a scenic but pricier water taxi. From Piazzale Roma or the Santa Lucia rail station, the historic center is on foot or by vaporetto — no cars enter the city at all.

Onward by rail, both high-speed: Florence about 2 hours, Rome about 3h45. Both make Venice a workable add-on to a northern-Italy leg rather than a destination requiring its own flight.
